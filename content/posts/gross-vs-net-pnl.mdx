**TL;DR:** Gross P&L is what your trades earned before charges. Net P&L is what stays after brokerage, STT, exchange fees, GST, stamp duty and slippage. Overtrading widens the gap because every round trip pays costs, win or lose. A strategy with a thin edge per trade can show a gross profit and still lose money net.

Your Kite P&L shows +₹18,000 for the month, and you feel good about it. Then you open the charges tab. Brokerage, STT, exchange fees and GST add up to ₹21,000, and your real result is −₹3,000. You didn't have a profitable month. You had a profitable-looking one, paid for by trading 40 times more than the plan called for.

## The Root Problem

Overtrading hurts because costs are charged per trade, not per outcome, so the more you trade, the more you pay regardless of whether you are right. Gross P&L hides this because it only shows what the market paid you.

Three things keep the gap invisible:

1. **Gross P&L is the headline number.** Broker dashboards and screenshots lead with the P&L figure, and charges sit one tab away.
2. **Each trade's cost feels trivial.** ₹120 on a trade is easy to ignore. ₹120 across 250 trades is ₹30,000.
3. **Slippage never appears anywhere.** It is not on the contract note. It shows up only as a worse fill price than you expected.

Here is how the same month looks through two lenses. The figures are hypothetical, and the cost per round trip is a placeholder to show the mechanics. Replace it with your broker's current numbers before publishing.

| | Disciplined month | Overtrading month |
|---|---|---|
| Trades (round trips) | 60 | 250 |
| Gross P&L | ₹22,000 | ₹27,000 |
| Charges per round trip (brokerage, STT, fees, GST, stamp duty) | ₹120 | ₹120 |
| Total charges | ₹7,200 | ₹30,000 |
| Estimated slippage (₹60 per round trip) | ₹3,600 | ₹15,000 |
| **Net P&L** | **₹11,200** | **−₹18,000** |

The overtrading month earned ₹5,000 more gross and finished ₹29,200 behind net. The extra 190 trades added ₹22,800 in charges and slippage, and they added only ₹5,000 of gross profit. This is the hidden ₹30,000: not one catastrophic loss, but the steady drip of costs on trades that had no edge.

## The Breakdown

Costs decide the outcome when the edge per trade is small compared with the friction per trade. The sections below break down where the money goes, how to find your breakeven, and how to measure your own gap.

### Where the Money Goes on an F&O Trade

An options round trip pays several separate layers of cost, and most of them apply to both winning and losing trades. Rates change, so always check your broker's current charge calculator and the exchange circulars.

| Cost layer | When it applies | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage | Per executed order | Flat per order at most discount brokers |
| STT | Sell side on options, charged on premium | Revised in past Union Budgets, so verify the current rate |
| Exchange transaction charges | Both sides, on turnover | Varies by segment |
| SEBI turnover fee | Both sides | Small but not zero |
| GST | On brokerage and exchange charges | Applied on fees, not on trade value |
| Stamp duty | Buy side | Check the current rate |
| Slippage | Every market or stop order | Hidden in your fill price |

[NEEDS SOURCE: current STT, exchange transaction charge, SEBI turnover fee and stamp duty rates for NSE index options]

Two features of this structure matter for overtraders. First, STT on options is charged on premium, so selling a premium of ₹150 and one of ₹15 are taxed very differently, which means cheap far-out-of-the-money scalps can carry a heavy cost relative to their profit. Second, fixed per-order brokerage hurts small trades more, because the same ₹20 takes a bigger bite from a ₹300 profit than from a ₹3,000 one.

### The Breakeven Edge per Trade

Your strategy survives only if the average gross profit per trade is greater than the average cost per trade, which makes cost per round trip your hurdle rate. It is the minimum edge you must clear before earning a rupee.

Take a strategy with a ₹180 average gross profit per trade and ₹150 of charges plus slippage per round trip:

| Trades per month | Gross P&L | Total friction | Net P&L |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | ₹7,200 | ₹6,000 | ₹1,200 |
| 100 | ₹18,000 | ₹15,000 | ₹3,000 |
| 250 | ₹45,000 | ₹37,500 | ₹7,500 |

Here, more trades scale the profit, but only because the edge per trade is positive after friction (₹30 net per trade). If slippage rises to ₹200 instead of ₹60 during volatile sessions, the same strategy goes negative. Edge that survives costs scales with trade count. Edge that doesn't, shrinks the account faster the more you trade.

That is why overtrading is dangerous even when each trade looks reasonable. Low-conviction trades have a lower average gross, but they pay the same friction. A simple test is to ask whether your average gross per trade is at least 2x your average cost per trade. If it is not, you have little room for error.

### Cutting the Cost Without Cutting the Edge

The goal is fewer low-conviction trades, not fewer trades for their own sake. Cut the trades whose gross edge can't clear the friction.

1. **Rank your setups by net profit per trade.** Drop or reduce the ones near zero or negative.
2. **Set a daily trade cap.** Pick a number from your own history, such as the trade count above which your net P&L turns negative.
3. **Avoid low-premium scalps where costs dominate.** If a ₹40 target on a ₹20 premium option can't clear friction, skip it.
4. **Size trades so the profit target is large relative to cost.** Use WickLog's Position Size and Risk-Reward calculators to check that a trade's reward is meaningfully bigger than its costs before you enter.
5. **Review monthly.** Compare net P&L on trade-heavy weeks with trade-light weeks. If the light weeks win, your cap is too high.

## Key Takeaways

- Gross P&L shows what the market paid you. Net P&L shows what you kept after charges and slippage.
- Costs are charged per trade, so overtrading raises them even when your strategy is unchanged.
- Your edge per trade must exceed your friction per trade. Check whether average gross is at least about 2x average cost.
- A month can look green gross and be red net. Always read both numbers.
- Import your real trades and compare net P&L on high-trade-count days with low-trade-count days to find your own cap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between gross P&L and net P&L in trading?

Gross P&L is your profit or loss from trades before any costs, and net P&L is what remains after brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, stamp duty and other fees. For active F&O traders the gap can be large, and slippage widens it further. Judge your performance on net P&L.

### How much do brokerage and STT cost on Nifty options trades?

It depends on your broker's flat fee, the premium traded and the current statutory rates, so there is no single figure. Use your broker's brokerage calculator for an exact number per trade, and check current STT and exchange rates before relying on any estimate. Costs are paid on every round trip, so frequent trading multiplies them.
