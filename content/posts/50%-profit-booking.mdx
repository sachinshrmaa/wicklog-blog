> **TL;DR:** Scaling out means booking profit on roughly half your position at your first target and letting the rest run with a trailing or breakeven stop. The math works because it converts a binary win/loss outcome into a partial-win-guaranteed structure, lowering the variance of your equity curve without necessarily lowering your expectancy.

You've been there: up 1.5R on a trade, it stalls, you hold for the "full target," and it round-trips back to your stop. You didn't lose money on a bad trade — you lost money on a *good* trade you managed like a coin flip. Scaling out exists specifically to stop this pattern, and the reason it works is arithmetic, not intuition.

## The Root Problem

The core issue with all-or-nothing exits is that they force every trade into one of two outcomes: full target or full stop, with nothing in between. This means your entire expectancy depends on correctly timing the exact top or bottom of a move — something even professional desks don't consistently do. Retail traders on NSE/BSE make this worse by holding full size through the entire move, so the psychological pressure to protect an open profit and the technical reality of "let winners run" are constantly in conflict.

Scaling out resolves this conflict structurally instead of relying on willpower. By booking 50% at a predefined first target, you lock in a partial win the moment the trade proves you right, and the remaining 50% is now effectively a "free" position — its downside is capped at breakeven (or better) if you move your stop up, while its upside is uncapped.

## The Breakdown

### The core formula: locked-in R vs. runner R

Assume a standard setup: you buy at ₹100, stop-loss at ₹98 (risk = ₹2/share = 1R), and first target at ₹104 (2R).

| Action | Shares | Exit Price | P&L on This Leg | Cumulative R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entry | 100 | ₹100 | — | 0R |
| Scale out 50% at target | 50 | ₹104 | +₹200 | +1R (locked) |
| Move stop to breakeven on remainder | 50 | ₹100 (stop) | ₹0 | +1R (worst case) |
| OR remainder runs to ₹110 | 50 | ₹110 | +₹500 | +3.5R (best case) |

The critical line is the "worst case" row. Without scaling out, a reversal after hitting ₹104 that comes back to your original ₹98 stop turns a +2R paper profit into a -1R realized loss — a 3R swing. With scaling out and a breakeven stop on the runner, the same reversal locks in +1R minimum. You've converted your worst-case outcome from a loss into a guaranteed win.

### Why this doesn't require a higher win rate to help your expectancy 

**Example (cash equity):**
- Account capital: ₹5,00,000
- Risk per trade: 1% = ₹5,000
- Entry: ₹250, Stop-loss: ₹240 (risk per share = ₹10)
- Position size = ₹5,000 / ₹10 = **500 shares**

Note what this formula does *not* ask: it doesn't ask how confident you are, how strong the setup looks, or how sure you are of the breakout. Your stop distance and your risk budget are the only two inputs — this is precisely why it removes emotion from sizing decisions.

**Example (F&O, Nifty options):**
- Account capital: ₹5,00,000
- Risk per trade: 1% = ₹5,000
- Premium at entry: ₹150, Stop-loss premium: ₹110 (risk per unit = ₹40)
- Nifty lot size: 75 (subject to NSE revision)
- Max lots by risk = ₹5,000 / (₹40 × 75) = 1.67 → **1 lot**

In F&O, lot size rounding often forces you *below* your full 1% allocation rather than above it — which is the safer direction to round, but worth knowing so you don't assume you're using your full risk budget when you're actually under it.

### Entry quality vs. position sizing: what each actually controls

| Factor | What It Controls | What It Doesn't Control |
|---|---|---|
| Entry timing/quality | Win rate, initial trade favorability | How much you lose if wrong |
| Stop-loss placement | Risk per share/unit | Whether the stop gets hit |
| Position sizing (1% rule) | Total capital at risk, survivability | Whether any single trade wins |

A perfect entry with oversized position risk still exposes your account to catastrophic single-trade loss. A mediocre entry with disciplined 1% sizing caps the damage regardless of outcome — this is why professional risk management prioritizes the sizing formula over entry refinement. ## Key Takeaways

- Risking 1% per trade means calculating your position size from your stop-loss distance, not from how much capital you feel like deploying.
- Drawdown recovery math is asymmetric — a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover, which is why small, consistent risk-per-trade matters more than any single entry decision.
- In F&O, lot size constraints often round your position size down below the full 1% budget — check this before assuming you're at full risk allocation.
- Entry quality determines whether you're right; position sizing determines what being wrong costs you — conflating the two is the most common retail sizing mistake.
- Calculate position size *before* you place the trade, not after you've already decided you like the setup — reversing this order is how sizing discipline quietly erodes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the 1% risk rule in trading?
The 1% risk rule means limiting your maximum potential loss on any single trade to 1% of your total trading capital, calculated from your entry price and stop-loss distance. It's a capital preservation rule, not a profit target — it caps downside without limiting upside.

### Is 1% risk per trade too conservative for Indian F&O traders?
Not necessarily — many experienced F&O traders on Zerodha, Groww, or Dhan use between 0.5% and 2% depending on their strategy's win rate and volatility exposure. 1% is a common default because it balances meaningful position size with survivability through losing streaks, but the exact number should be back-tested against your own strategy's historical drawdowns.

### How do I calculate position size for options trading using the 1% rule?
Divide your risk amount (1% of capital) by your risk per unit (entry premium minus stop-loss premium), then divide by the lot size to get the number of lots. Lot size rounding usually rounds your position down rather than up, so always round to the nearest whole lot below your calculated number, never above.
